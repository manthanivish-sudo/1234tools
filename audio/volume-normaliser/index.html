Normalise a recording ↑

What is loudness normalisation?

Normalising sets a recording to a standard loudness, so it plays at the same level as everything around it. The old way aimed the highest peak at full scale; the modern way, used by broadcasters and streaming services, aims the perceived loudness, measured in LUFS, at a target.

Loudness and peak are different things: a recording with sharp drum hits can peak high and still sound quiet, which is why peak normalising leaves podcasts and songs at such different levels.

How it works in your browser

Loudness is measured to ITU-R BS.1770-4 in a worker, then one gain is applied.

  1. The sound is K-weighted (a high shelf for the head and a low cut), and its power is averaged over 400 ms blocks that overlap by three quarters.
  2. Blocks quieter than −70 LUFS are ignored, then those more than 10 LU below the average of the rest; what remains gives the integrated loudness.
  3. The true peak is found by oversampling four times, so peaks between samples count.
  4. The gain is the target minus the measurement, cut back if it would lift the true peak above −1 dBTP.

Another worked example

A 1 kHz sine at −30 dBFS measured −30.0 LUFS, as the EBU test signals predict; set to −16 LUFS it was raised by 14.0 dB and measured −16.0 LUFS afterwards, its peak at −16.007 dBFS.

Common uses

  • Audiobooks Keep every chapter at one level, as audiobook stores expect.
  • Background music for video Set the music to a known level before mixing it under a voice.
  • Comparing takes Bring two takes to one loudness so the better one is judged fairly.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Aiming a very dynamic classical recording at −14 LUFS. Its peaks stop it short; the page says how far, and a lower target suits it better.
  • Normalising each half of a recording separately, then joining them. Join first, then normalise once.

More questions

How do dBFS and LU relate?

Both use decibel steps. dBFS is a level against digital full scale; LU is a step of BS.1770 loudness. Raising a file by 1 dB raises its loudness by 1 LU.

Why −1 dBTP and not 0?

Converting to AAC or Opus, or playing through a phone’s output, can push peaks a little higher; a decibel of room stops that from clipping.

Does it change the tone of the sound?

No. One gain is applied to every sample; nothing is filtered, compressed or limited.

Privacy

Nothing you add is uploaded. Your file is decoded by your browser’s own audio decoder, measured and adjusted in this tab. The target, format and bitrate are remembered in this browser; the file and its name are not kept. There is no account.

How it works

  1. Choose a sound file or a video. Its integrated loudness, true peak and sample peak are measured straight away.
  2. Choose a target: −14 LUFS for music streaming, −16 for podcasts, −19 for quieter speech, −23 for EBU R128 broadcast, or the loudest peak at −1 dBTP.
  3. Read how many decibels it will be raised or lowered by, then press Normalise.
  4. The result is measured again; the before-and-after readings are shown under it.

What people use it for

  • Podcasts — Bring every episode to −16 LUFS so listeners never reach for the volume.
  • Uploads to streaming sites — Hear your track at the level the service will play it.
  • Mixed recordings — Even out clips from different microphones before joining them.
  • Teaching material — A quiet lecture recording, brought up to a level a phone speaker can carry.

Tips

  • LUFS measures loudness as people hear it, not the highest peak, which is why two files that peak at the same level can sound very different.
  • One gain changes the level only: the quiet and loud parts keep their distance from each other.
  • When the target would push the peaks over −1 dBTP the gain stops there, and the page says how loud the result is instead.
  • A file that is already at its target is left within a few hundredths of a decibel of where it was.

Limits and browser support

Files up to 400 MB and 3 hours; the sound is held decoded in memory, so on a phone keep files short.

No compressor or limiter is applied, so a very dynamic recording may stop short of a loud target; the page says by how much.

Frequently asked questions

What is LUFS?

Loudness units relative to full scale: a measure of how loud a recording sounds over its whole length, from ITU-R BS.1770. It weights the frequencies the ear is most sensitive to and leaves out silent passages, so it matches what people hear far better than the peak level does.

Which target should I use?

Music for streaming: −14 LUFS. Podcasts: −16 LUFS. Broadcast in Europe: −23 LUFS (EBU R128). For quiet, dynamic speech, −19 keeps more headroom.

Will it make my audio distort?

No. The gain is never allowed to take the true peak — the highest point of the wave between samples — above −1 dBTP, so nothing clips.

Does it compress the sound?

No. It applies one gain to the whole file, so the dynamics are exactly as recorded.

How accurate is the measurement?

It follows ITU-R BS.1770-4 (K-weighting, 400 ms blocks, the −70 LUFS and −10 LU gates) and reads the EBU Tech 3341 test signals within 0.1 LU.

Is my file uploaded?

No. It is measured and adjusted in this tab.

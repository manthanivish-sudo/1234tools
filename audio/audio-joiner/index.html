Audio Joiner

Join sound files into one in your browser: put them in order, add a gap of silence or a crossfade between them, and save as WAV, M4A or Opus. Mixed sample rates and mono files are matched for you. Nothing is uploaded.

What is joining audio files?

Joining puts several recordings end to end in one file. Files rarely share a format: one may be a 44.1 kHz mono voice memo, another a 48 kHz stereo music track, and they have to be brought to one sample rate and one channel layout before they can sit side by side.

What goes between them matters too. Speech wants a short gap; music usually wants a crossfade, where one track fades out as the next fades in.

How it works in your browser

Every file is decoded first; then the highest sample rate among them becomes the rate of the result.

  1. Files at a lower rate are resampled up with a windowed-sinc filter; mono files are copied to both channels when any file is stereo.
  2. A gap is written as silence of the length chosen.
  3. A crossfade overlaps the end of one file with the start of the next along equal-power curves, cosine out and sine in, so the level holds through it.
  4. The whole result is then saved as WAV, M4A or Opus.

Another worked example

A 4-second 44.1 kHz mono test tone and a 6-second 48 kHz stereo one, joined with a 1-second gap, made an 11-second 48 kHz stereo WAV. In reverse order with a 1-second crossfade the result lasted 9 seconds, and the level in the middle of the fade was 0.212, the same as either side.

Common uses

  • Wedding and event playlists One file of the first-dance songs that plays without gaps.
  • Language lessons Join short drills into one track for practice.
  • Field recordings Stitch the clips a recorder split at its file-size limit.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Joining files of very different loudness. Even them out first with the Volume Normaliser, or the change jars.
  • Crossfading speech. Words in the overlap become hard to follow; use a gap instead.

More questions

Does joining lose quality?

Not as WAV: every file is decoded and placed as it is, apart from resampling the lower-rate ones. M4A and Opus are encoded once, at the end.

What sample rate does the result use?

The highest among the files. Lower-rate files are resampled up, which keeps the better files intact.

Why equal-power crossfades?

Fading two different sounds in straight lines makes the middle of the fade sound quieter; cosine and sine curves keep the combined power steady.

Privacy

Nothing you add is uploaded. Your files are decoded by your browser’s own audio decoder and joined in this tab. What goes between them, the format and the bitrate are remembered in this browser; the files and their names are not kept. There is no account.

How it works

  1. Choose two or more sound files, or videos whose sound you want.
  2. Put them in order with the ↑ and ↓ buttons; × leaves one out.
  3. Choose what goes between them: nothing, half a second to two seconds of silence, or a crossfade of one to three seconds.
  4. Choose WAV, M4A or Opus and press Join the files.

What people use it for

  • Podcast episodes — Put the intro, the interview and the outro together in one file.
  • Mixtapes and playlists — One continuous file of songs with crossfades between them.
  • Audiobook chapters — Join the chapter files of a recording into one.
  • Voice notes — Several short recordings of one conversation, as one file to send.

Tips

  • A crossfade suits music; a short gap of silence suits speech.
  • Files at a lower sample rate are resampled to the highest rate among them, so nothing is lost from the best file.
  • If any file is stereo the result is stereo; mono files are spread to both sides.
  • Join as WAV while you are still editing, then make an M4A or Opus at the end.

Limits and browser support

Up to 400 MB and 3 hours a file, and the joined sound is held in memory, so on a phone keep the total short.

There is no MP3 output: the usual MP3 encoder (LAME) is under the LGPL, which this site does not ship.

M4A and Opus need WebCodecs’ audio encoder; where a browser lacks it, WAV still works.

Frequently asked questions

Can I join files of different formats?

Yes. An MP3, a WAV and an M4A can be joined: each is decoded first, then they are matched to one sample rate and channel count.

How does the crossfade work?

The end of one file fades out while the start of the next fades in, over the length you choose, along equal-power curves so the loudness does not dip in the middle. A file too short for the crossfade gets one half its length.

Can I change the order?

Yes: the ↑ and ↓ buttons move a file, and × takes it out.

Can I save it as MP3?

No, for licence reasons. WAV, M4A and Opus play everywhere MP3 does.

Is there a limit on how many files?

No set number; the limit is memory, since the joined sound is built in the page.

Are my files uploaded?

No. They are decoded and joined in this tab.

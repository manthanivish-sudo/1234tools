How to calculate CAGR

CAGR = (end value ÷ start value)^(1 ÷ years) − 1. An investment that grew from £10,000 to £18,000 in five years has a CAGR of 1.8^(1 ÷ 5) − 1 = 12.47% a year — the steady yearly rate that would have produced the same result, whatever actually happened in between.

7 min read · 5 steps · 5 ways it goes wrong

What you need before you start

  • The value at the start and the value at the end, measured the same way — both revenue for a full year, or both the market value of the same holding.
  • The exact time between the two measurements, in years. Six months is 0.5 of a year.
  • Whether money was added or taken out along the way. If it was, CAGR is the wrong tool and XIRR is the right one.

1. Count the years between the two values

The number of years is the gap between the two measurements, not the number of years named. Revenue of £250,000 in 2021 and £400,000 in 2025 spans four years of growth — 2021 to 2022, 2022 to 2023, 2023 to 2024 and 2024 to 2025 — even though five years appear in the range.

For anything measured on dates rather than in whole years, convert the gap to a decimal: seven years and six months is 7.5.

2. Divide, take the root, subtract one

CAGR = (end ÷ start)^(1 ÷ years) − 1

Divide the end value by the start value: 18,000 ÷ 10,000 = 1.8. Raise that to the power of one over the number of years — 1.8 to the power 0.2 is about 1.1247. Subtract 1 and turn it into a percentage: 12.47% a year.

In a spreadsheet, =(18000/10000)^(1/5)-1 gives the same answer, and so does =RRI(5, 10000, 18000).

The tool for this step: CAGR Calculator (Compound Annual Growth Rate) — works out the compound annual growth rate, the total growth, the growth multiple and the years to double, and projects the value forward at the same rate.

Do it in the CAGR calculator with £10,000 to £18,000 over five years — it shows 12.475%

3. Check it by running it forwards

Multiply the start value by (1 + CAGR) once for every year: 10,000 × 1.1247 × 1.1247 × 1.1247 × 1.1247 × 1.1247 comes back to £18,000. If it does not, the number of years is wrong.

The rule of 72 is a quick sense check: 72 ÷ 12.47 suggests the money doubles in about 5.8 years; the exact figure, which the calculator gives, is 5.9 years. Going forwards, the same rate would take £18,000 to £20,245.43 a year later and £25,611.56 after three — if, and only if, the rate held.

4. Why the average of yearly growth rates is the wrong answer

Something that rises 50% in one year and falls 50% the next has an average yearly growth of zero. But 100 became 150 and then 75, so a quarter of the money has gone. CAGR says so: (75 ÷ 100)^(1 ÷ 2) − 1 = −13.40% a year. Averaging percentages that compound always flatters the result, and the swings decide by how much.

5. Know what CAGR leaves out

  • The path. A steady 12% a year and a lurching ride that ends in the same place have the same CAGR, and very different risks.
  • The choice of dates. Starting the count in a bad year, or ending it in a good one, can change the answer more than anything that happened in between.
  • Money in and out. A balance that grew partly because more was paid in has not grown at its CAGR. For regular contributions, use XIRR.

Where this usually goes wrong

5 things that actually happen, rather than a note asking you to be careful.

  • Counting the years in the range instead of the gaps. Revenue from 2021 to 2025 is four years of growth. Dividing by five turns £250,000 to £400,000 into 9.86% a year instead of 12.47%, and the error grows the shorter the period is.
  • Averaging the yearly growth rates. An arithmetic average of +50% and −50% is zero; the money actually fell by a quarter. Use the start and end values, not the average of the steps between them.
  • Choosing a flattering start year. Measuring from the bottom of a slump makes any recovery look like fast growth. When you quote a CAGR, give the dates, and when you read one, check whether a different start year would tell a different story.
  • Using CAGR when money was added along the way. A portfolio that went from £10,000 to £18,000 partly because £5,000 was paid in has not grown at 12.47% a year. CAGR assumes one sum at the start and nothing else; XIRR handles deposits and withdrawals on their dates.
  • Reading the projection as a forecast. The projected values assume the same rate carries on. They show what the rate means in money, not what will happen next year.

How long this should take

Seconds once you have the two values and the right number of years. Counting the years is where most wrong answers come from, so spend the minute there.

Frequently asked questions

What is the CAGR formula?

CAGR = (end value ÷ start value)^(1 ÷ number of years) − 1, multiplied by 100 for a percentage. It is the geometric mean growth rate: the single yearly rate that turns the start value into the end value.

How do I calculate CAGR in Excel?

Use =(end/start)^(1/years)-1 and format the cell as a percentage, or =RRI(years, start, end). For £10,000 to £18,000 over five years, both give 12.47%.

Can CAGR be negative?

Yes. If the end value is lower than the start value the rate is negative: £100 to £75 over two years is −13.40% a year.

What is the difference between CAGR and total growth?

Total growth is the whole change over the period: £10,000 to £18,000 is 80%. CAGR spreads it into a yearly rate, 12.47%, so that periods of different lengths can be compared.

What counts as a good CAGR?

It depends entirely on what is being measured and the alternative. Compare it with something real over the same dates — inflation, a savings rate, or an index — rather than with a number in isolation.

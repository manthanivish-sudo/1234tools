How to calculate BMI

BMI is your weight in kilograms divided by the square of your height in metres: 70 kg at 1.75 m is 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 22.9. For adults, the WHO counts under 18.5 as underweight, 25 or more as overweight and 30 or more as obese — but BMI is a quick screening number, not a diagnosis, and a doctor or nurse is the person to interpret it for you.

8 min read · 5 steps · 5 ways it goes wrong

What you need before you start

  • Your weight, measured without shoes and in light clothing. Morning, before eating, gives the most repeatable figure.
  • Your height, measured standing straight against a wall without shoes.
  • If the BMI is for a child or teenager, their age and sex as well — the adult categories do not apply to them.

1. Get both measurements into the same system

The metric formula needs kilograms and metres. Height is usually measured in centimetres, so divide by 100: 175 cm is 1.75 m. If you measure in pounds and inches, use the imperial version of the formula instead, which has a conversion factor built in.

metric: BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²

imperial: BMI = 703 × weight (lb) ÷ height (in)²

2. Square the height, then divide

Multiply the height by itself: 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625. Divide the weight by that: 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.86. Round to one decimal place for everyday use: 22.9.

In imperial: someone who is 5 ft 9 in (69 inches) and 154 lb has a BMI of 703 × 154 ÷ (69 × 69) = 703 × 154 ÷ 4,761 = 22.7. The calculator converts pounds and inches exactly rather than using the 703 shortcut, and gets the same 22.7.

3. Read the result against the adult categories

The figures here, and when they were last checked

For adults, the World Health Organization defines underweight as a BMI below 18.5, overweight as a BMI of 25 or more, and obesity as a BMI of 30 or more, which leaves 18.5 to just under 25 as the healthy range. For children aged 5 to 19 it does not use these cut-offs at all: overweight and obesity are defined by BMI-for-age against the WHO Growth Reference, at more than one and more than two standard deviations above the median. Under the age of five, weight-for-height against the WHO Child Growth Standards is used instead.

  • World Health Organization — Global Health Observatory: body mass index — underweight among adults, BMI below 18.5

4. For a child or teenager, use BMI-for-age instead

Children’s bodies change shape as they grow, so the same BMI means different things at 6 and at 16, and different things for girls and boys. The number is still worked out the same way, but it is read against a growth chart for the child’s exact age and sex, as a percentile or a standard-deviation score. The adult bands of 18.5, 25 and 30 should never be applied to a child. A health visitor, school nurse or GP can plot it on the right chart.

5. Know where BMI gives the wrong picture

  • Muscular people and athletes: muscle is heavier than fat, so BMI overstates fatness.
  • Older adults: muscle is often lost with age, so a normal BMI can hide too little muscle and too much fat.
  • Pregnancy: weight gain is expected, so a BMI worked out during pregnancy does not mean what it would otherwise. Ask the midwife.
  • Ethnicity: health risk can rise at a lower BMI in some populations, and some health services use lower thresholds for people of certain ethnic backgrounds — ask what your own doctor uses.
  • Where fat is carried: two people with the same BMI can carry it very differently. Waist measurement says something BMI cannot.

If your BMI is outside the healthy range, or you are worried about your weight for any reason, talk to a doctor, nurse or pharmacist. They will look at much more than one number.

Where this usually goes wrong

5 things that actually happen, rather than a note asking you to be careful.

  • Leaving the height in centimetres, or not squaring it. Dividing 70 by 175 squared gives 0.0023, which is obviously wrong. The subtler slip is forgetting to square: 70 ÷ 1.75 = 40, which can pass for an answer at a glance. Convert centimetres to metres, and square the height, not the weight.
  • Using the adult chart for a child. A BMI of 17 might be healthy for one ten-year-old and a concern for another, depending on age and sex. Adult categories applied to children mislabel them in both directions. Children need BMI-for-age on a growth chart.
  • Treating the boundary as a cliff. A BMI of 24.9 and one of 25.1 describe almost exactly the same body. The cut-offs are lines drawn for population statistics and screening, not a point at which health changes. Look at the trend over time rather than which side of a line one reading falls.
  • Reading a muscular build as overweight. BMI cannot tell muscle from fat. A rugby forward at 185 cm and 95 kg has a BMI of 27.8 and may be very lean. For anyone who trains hard, body composition or waist measurement says far more.
  • Comparing readings from different scales. Bathroom scales disagree with each other by a kilogram or more, and weight moves through the day. A change in BMI from one set of scales to another can be entirely the scales. Use the same scales at the same time of day.

How long this should take

Seconds once you have an accurate height and weight. Measuring properly — shoes off, same scales, same time of day — is the part that makes the number worth having.

Frequently asked questions

What is a healthy BMI for adults?

The WHO adult range for a healthy weight runs from 18.5 to just under 25. It is a screening range for adults, not a target set for any individual, and it does not apply to children.

How do I calculate BMI in pounds and inches?

Multiply your weight in pounds by 703, then divide by your height in inches squared. At 154 lb and 69 inches that is 703 × 154 ÷ 4,761 = 22.7.

Is BMI accurate for athletes?

Not very. It counts all weight the same, so muscle pushes it up. An athlete can be in the overweight band with low body fat. Body composition measurements are a better guide.

What BMI is right for my child?

There is no single number. A child’s BMI is compared with others of the same age and sex on a growth chart. Ask a health visitor, school nurse or GP to plot it.

My BMI is above 25. Should I worry?

Not on the strength of one number. Speak to a doctor, nurse or pharmacist, who will consider your waist measurement, blood pressure, activity, family history and more before saying anything about your health.

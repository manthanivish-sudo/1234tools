How to calculate a percentage

To find what percentage one number is of another, divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100: 36 out of 48 is 36 ÷ 48 × 100 = 75%. To take a percentage of an amount, multiply the amount by the percentage and divide by 100: 15% of 1,200 is 1,200 × 15 ÷ 100 = 180.

8 min read · 5 steps · 5 ways it goes wrong

What you need before you start

  • The two numbers, and a clear idea of which one is the whole. Nearly every wrong percentage comes from dividing by the wrong one.
  • For a change over time, the old value and the new value, in that order. The old value is always the one you divide by.
  • A calculator, or the Percentage Calculator, which gives all six readings of the same two numbers at once so you can pick the one you meant.

1. Decide which percentage question you are asking

Every everyday percentage is one of three questions, and each has its own one-line formula. Getting the question right is most of the job.

  • What percentage is one number of another? 36 marks out of 48, 18 days absent out of 220.
  • What is a given percentage of an amount? 15% of 1,200, 18% GST on a price, a 10% tip.
  • By what percentage did something change? A price that went from 1,200 to 1,380, a score that fell from 80 to 60.

A fourth question — what was the number before a percentage was added or taken off — is the one people most often get wrong, and it has its own step below.

2. Work out what percentage one number is of another

percentage = part ÷ whole × 100

Divide the part by the whole, then multiply by 100. For 36 marks out of 48: 36 ÷ 48 = 0.75, and 0.75 × 100 = 75%. The whole is the total that the part belongs to — the maximum marks, the total number of days, the full bill.

A quick check that saves embarrassment: if the part is smaller than the whole, the answer must be under 100%. If you get 133%, you have divided the wrong way round.

3. Work out a percentage of an amount — and add or take it off

amount × percentage ÷ 100

15% of 1,200 is 1,200 × 15 ÷ 100 = 180. To increase 1,200 by 15%, add that on — 1,380 — or do it in one go by multiplying by 1.15. To reduce 1,200 by 15%, multiply by 0.85 and get 1,020.

In your head, build from 10% by moving the decimal point one place left: 10% of 1,200 is 120, 5% is 60, so 15% is 180.

Open it with 15% and 1,200 — it shows 180, 1,380 and 1,020

4. Work out a percentage increase or decrease

percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100

Subtract the old value from the new one, divide by the old value, multiply by 100. A price that went from 1,200 to 1,380 rose by 180, and 180 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 15%. A score that fell from 80 to 60 changed by −20, and −20 ÷ 80 × 100 = −25%: a fall of 25%.

The direction matters. Going back up from 60 to 80 is a rise of 20 ÷ 60 = 33.3%, not 25%, because the starting point is smaller — which is why a 25% fall needs a 33.3% rise to undo it.

When neither number is the starting point — two shops’ prices, two candidates’ scores — use percentage difference: the gap divided by the average of the two. 36 and 48 differ by 12 and average 42, so they are 28.57% apart either way round.

The tool for this step: Percentage Calculator — with the old value as A and the new value as B, the “% change from A to B” line is the answer, signed.

Check it with 1,200 and 1,380 already in — it shows a 15% change

5. Reverse a percentage to find the original number

after a rise: original = final ÷ (1 + percentage ÷ 100)

after a cut: original = final ÷ (1 − percentage ÷ 100)

If a price is 540 after a 20% rise, the original is 540 ÷ 1.20 = 450. It is not 540 minus 20% (432), because the 20% was worked out on 450, not on 540. If a sale price is 360 after 25% off, the full price was 360 ÷ 0.75 = 480.

Check a reversed percentage by running it forwards: put 20 and 450 into the Percentage Calculator and “B increased by A%” reads 540. The same division takes tax out of a price that includes it.

Where this usually goes wrong

5 things that actually happen, rather than a note asking you to be careful.

  • Dividing by the wrong number. The whole goes on the bottom: marks scored over marks available, not the other way round. For a change, the old value goes on the bottom, not the new one — 1,200 to 1,380 is 15% up, but dividing by 1,380 gives 13%, which is a different and wrong answer to the question asked.
  • Taking a percentage off to undo one that was added. A price of 540 that includes a 20% rise came from 450, not 432. Subtracting 20% of the final figure removes too much, because the rise was a percentage of the smaller original. Divide by 1.20 instead. The same error, with tax, understates the taxable value on every invoice it touches.
  • Adding percentages that happen one after another. A 20% fall followed by a 20% rise does not get you back where you started: 100 becomes 80, and 80 plus 20% is 96, so you are 4% down. Two 10% rises in a row make 21%, not 20%. Successive percentages multiply; they do not add.
  • Confusing percent with percentage points. If an interest rate goes from 5% to 7%, it has risen by 2 percentage points — and by 40% in relative terms. Both are true, and a headline can pick whichever sounds bigger.
  • Rounding in the middle of the sum. Round only the final answer. Rounding 36 ÷ 48 to 0.8 before multiplying by 100 turns 75% into 80%.

How long this should take

Seconds, once you have decided which of the three questions you are asking — which is the only part that takes thought. If a percentage answer looks wrong, it is almost never the arithmetic; it is the wrong number on the bottom of the fraction.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for percentage?

Part divided by whole, multiplied by 100. For a percentage of an amount, it runs the other way: amount multiplied by the percentage, divided by 100. For a change, the difference divided by the old value, multiplied by 100.

How do I calculate percentage increase between two numbers?

Take the old number from the new one, divide by the old number and multiply by 100. From 1,200 to 1,380 is 180 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 15%. A negative answer means it went down.

How do I work out a percentage of marks?

Marks obtained divided by maximum marks, times 100: 36 out of 48 is 75%. For papers with different maximums, add up all the marks and all the maximums first, then divide; averaging the separate percentages gives a different answer.

What is the difference between percentage change and percentage difference?

Percentage change has a direction and a starting point: it divides by the old value. Percentage difference has neither: it divides the gap by the average of the two numbers, so it gives the same answer whichever way round you put them. 36 and 48 are 28.57% apart; going from 36 to 48 is a 33.33% increase.
